QUANTUM COMPUTING • SESSION 04

Measurement & Probability

What happens when we finally look at a qubit? Understand measurement, probabilities, repeated experiments, and why quantum answers are not usually deterministic.

🎯

Session Goal

Connect a quantum state to measurable outcomes and build an intuitive understanding of probability through repeated measurements.

🧠

Key Question

If a qubit is prepared in a superposition, why does one measurement give a definite classical result?

🔑

Key Terms

Measurement • Probability • Amplitude • Basis • Outcome • Repeated trials

📖 Story — The Mystery Box

“There is a mysterious box. Every time you run the experiment, the box gives one visible answer. But when you repeat the experiment many times, a pattern appears.”

Connect this to a qubit. A quantum state contains information that lets us calculate probabilities for different measurement outcomes. When we measure in the computational basis, a single qubit produces either 0 or 1. The probability model predicts the distribution we see over many repeated preparations and measurements.

Imagine a quiz machine that has a hidden quantum state. Before measurement, we describe the state using amplitudes. When we measure it, the machine gives us a classical result.

For a state |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩, measurement in the computational basis gives:

P(0) = |α|²     and     P(1) = |β|²

with |α|² + |β|² = 1

🎯 Example — A Qubit with an 80/20 Distribution

Suppose:

|ψ⟩ = √0.8|0⟩ + √0.2|1⟩

Then each measurement produces one outcome, but repeated measurements should approach this distribution:

These percentages describe the probability predicted by the quantum state, not a guarantee about any single measurement.

⚠️ Important Misconception

Wrong: “Quantum computers give random answers.”

Better: A quantum algorithm prepares and transforms a quantum state so that measurement has a useful probability distribution. The algorithm is designed so that desired answers can be made more likely or otherwise extracted efficiently.

Example: In a successful quantum algorithm, we may repeat the circuit many times and identify the outcome that appears with high probability.

🎮 Classroom Activity — Quantum Coin Experiment

  1. Imagine a qubit prepared with a 50% probability of measuring 0 and 50% probability of measuring 1.
  2. Students predict the result of 10 measurements.
  3. Collect everyone's predicted sequences on the board.
  4. Compare the different sequences.
  5. Discuss: “Can two correct experiments produce different sequences?”
  6. Combine the class results and calculate the overall frequency of 0 and 1.

Learning point: Individual outcomes can vary even when the underlying probability distribution is the same.
